Steering a shell that spins 270 times per second

“Controller Design and Implementation for a Guidance Kit Used at Spinning Rockets and Projectiles” — Görkem Günay’s 2022 METU master’s thesis (supervisors: Prof. Dr. Raif Tuna Balkan & Assist. Prof. Dr. Hakan Çalışkan, with industry work at Roketsan). It asks one question: can you guide a cheap, spin-stabilized artillery shell without servos, complicated electronics, or a battery? The answer it builds and proves on a real test rig: yes — by turning the whole actuation problem into holding the roll angle of an alternator.

The whole thesis in three moves

If you read nothing else, read this — then everything below is detail.

Reference only: this page explains the source thesis and its flight/test-rig architecture. The active workspace artifact is a separate inert CAD demonstrator (`PGK_155mm.FCStd`): de-spun stator + 4 fixed strakes, ONE 61811 bearing, ring motor (torque ⇄ regen), detent launch lock. Do not treat this page as a fabrication or launch specification.
1 · Isolate

A ring of 4 fixed canards sits on the nose, mounted on bearings so it doesn’t spin with the body. Two “steering” canards create a net side-force; two “control” canards create a torque that fights the spin. No moving surfaces, no servo bandwidth problem.

2 · Load a generator

The actuator is a permanent-magnet alternator: its stator spins with the body, its rotor holds the canards. Drawing load current brakes the rotor against the body spin. The entire actuation problem collapses to one-axis position control — and the alternator doubles as the power supply, so no battery.

3 · Steer by roll angle

The side-force always points in the kit’s roll direction θgk. Hold θgk “up” and the shell lifts; rotate it and the force rotates. A cascade P + PI controller holds that angle — and re-tunes its own saturation limits in flight using a disturbance observer, because aerodynamic torque collapses to ~0 near apogee.

Why it matters: unguided spin-stabilized shells need adjustment fire (“shoot–observe–shoot”) — slow and risky. Missile-grade guidance needs servos, batteries and complex drivers that must survive 15,000 g. This kit is the cheap middle path: fixed aerodynamics + a generator + one control loop. The same fixed-canard concept was patented as US 6,981,672 (2006); this thesis contributes the rig, the model, the controller and the flight-proof of control authority.

Why spin-stabilized shells miss

Chapter 1 · pp. 1–8 — motivation, literature survey, contributions

A spin-stabilized projectile is a gyroscope: rifling in the barrel spins it fast enough that aerodynamic wobble can’t tumble it. That stability is exactly what makes it blind and unsteerable — and any guidance hardware must live through the launch (15,000 g axial, 5,000 g radial) and then function while the world rotates around it up to 300 times a second.

The thesis surveys the two existing correction approaches:

this thesis		Fixed surfaces on a bearing-isolated nose; an alternator is both the actuator and the generator.One control axis — but you only steer when aerodynamic torque beats friction (the whole challenge of Ch. 6).

The trick: four fixed canards and an alternator

Controllability is a torque tug-of-war

Equations 5.1–5.2 of the thesis. The kit can only be turned “with the spin” by braking less, and “against the spin” only if the wind wins:

T+x = Talt + Tf − Taero  → turning with the spin: always possible (load the alternator) T−x = −(Talt + Tf) + Taero  → turning against: needs Taero > Tf

What “the same physics, scaled” means

A real shell spins at ~15,500 rpm — far beyond the rig. Chapter 6 scales the body spin but applies the aerodynamic torque unchanged:

prelative,max = 80 V / Kv = 106.6 rad/s pbody,max = 106.6 − 31.42 = 75.18 rad/s C = 1623.2 / 75.18 = 21.6× down-scaling → test max body spin = 800 rpm (83.8 rad/s)

Consequence to remember: on the rig the kit-to-body speed ratio is 21.6× larger than in flight — which is exactly why one controller variant overshoots on the rig but would work on the real shell (Ch. 7). The thesis is refreshingly honest about this.

Software: host PC LabVIEW UI for setpoints/scopes; FPGA target decodes the encoder, runs the 1 kHz control law with saturation + anti-windup and picks the rotation direction that demands minimum control effort (a roll angle is circular — you may rotate either way; Ch. 4.4).

How each choice was forced by the math

Why PI in the velocity loop?

Final Value Theorem on the step error: zero steady-state error needs a free integrator (Kp = ∞). The same integrator drives step-disturbance error from Taero and pbody to zero (Eq. 4.13, 4.15).

Why P-only in the position loop?

The inner loop’s integrator already makes the position open-loop type-1 — Kp = ∞ for free. Disturbances are handled below. Adding more would only risk stability.

Why clamping anti-windup?

The velocity command saturates at the load’s current limit; a naive integrator keeps accumulating during saturation → overshoot on release. Clamping freezes the integrator exactly when pushing further into saturation.

Why “minimum control effort”?

θgk is circular. For a 350° error, rotating −10° is cheaper than +350°. The FPGA picks the shorter way (Fig. 4.14).

Hardware validation (Section 4.4): step tests and a chirp (0.01 → 7.5 Hz over 15 s, amplitude π/8) match simulation; measured cut-off 6.23 Hz vs. designed 6.2 Hz. Residual test-only wobble at standstill is traced to encoder velocity resolution (24 rpm) and non-linear Coulomb friction.

Experiment

Things to discover: (1) negative targets ride the wind passively — the load can only brake “with” the spin; (2) switch anti-windup off and watch overshoot appear; (3) push the wind past ≈1.46 N·m (Kt·1.9 A + friction) and no gain can hold position — this is Chapter 6’s “lost control authority”, not bad tuning.

The 6-DOF model (Eq. 5.7–5.10) integrates forces/moments — lift, drag, Magnus, pitch/yaw moments & damping, roll damping, gravity (all in Appendix G) — in the non-rolling frame. Test case (Table 5.1): 155 mm, 43 kg, Ixx = 0.144, Iyy = Izz = 1.216 kg·m², V₀ = 800 m/s, spin 1600 rad/s, 63° elevation.

The 7-DOF extension (Eq. 5.11–5.14) adds one rotational DOF: the free-spinning kit (pgk decoupled from pbody, separate inertia Ixgk). This is what produces the two numbers the rig needs: Taero(t) on the canards and pbody(t).

The flight-long torque story (stylized from Figs. 6.1–6.2)

Aerodynamic torque is born and dies with dense air and speed: huge at launch, ≈0 at apogee, large again on descent. Wherever |Taero| drops below friction, the kit can’t be turned against the spin — control authority is a window, not a constant. The thesis warns: if friction beats aero at apogee, guidance simply must wait until after it.

Honest caveat in the data: the aero-coefficient set exists only at Mach 0.9, 0.95, 1.1, 1.5, 2.0 — so the torque traces contain sudden unphysical jumps where the tables interpolate. The thesis flags these explicitly (Section 5.3.1).

Chapter 6 — the controller that re-tunes itself mid-flight

pp. 81–94 — flight scenario, failure of the fixed controller, disturbance observer, three fixes

Step 1: watch the fixed controller fail

Feeding the rig the real Taero(t) (unscaled) and pbody(t) (scaled 21.6×), with a square-wave reference of amplitude π/4 through the whole flight, the original controller loses control between t ≈ 30–50 s — around apogee, where the wind dies and can no longer turn the kit against friction. Overshoots grow until the system is effectively open-loop.

Step 2: observe the total disturbance

A frequency-domain disturbance observer estimates the combined wind + friction as an equivalent current Î — using only signals that exist (you can’t measure load voltage and current simultaneously, so the time-domain/observer-with-all-states route is out). Plant inverse needs a filter to stay causal:

Ĝ = 588.4s + 1.04  reduced plant (Appendix F) Q = 10.5s + 1  1.1 s rise — slow on purpose; T_aero has no high frequencies

Discretized for the FPGA (c2d, first-order hold, 1 ms): Gobs(z) = (0.003397z − 0.003394)/(z − 0.998), Q(z) = (0.0009993z + 0.0009987)/(z − 0.998).

Why the rig behaves worse than the sim (Ch. 7 honesty list): DC-load response dynamics, continuous-vs-discrete differences, fixed-point rounding on the FPGA, non-linear Coulomb friction, and — the big one — the alternator simply cannot make current at low relative speed, so demanded braking torque vanishes exactly when the scaled-down body spin makes the kit relatively fast. Chapter 8 — verdict and what’s next

pp. 103–105

The thesis proves, in order

  1. A fixed-canard + alternator kit reduces 2-D guidance actuation to one-axis position control — and powers itself.
  2. A bench rig can reproduce flight torque and (scaled) spin, and its 3-state model matches hardware within a few percent.
  3. Classical cascade P/PI with clamping anti-windup meets every requirement in hardware (6.23 Hz measured vs 6.2 Hz designed).
  4. 7-DOF flight sim delivers the real Taero(t) and pbody(t); control authority vanishes near apogee — a physics constraint, not a tuning flaw.
  5. A disturbance observer + dynamic saturation limits keep the system controllable, overshoot-free and symmetric through the whole flight — verified on hardware.

Declared future work

  • Extend the flight model: Earth curvature, altitude-dependent gravity, Mach-only aero tables refined, incidence/sideslip dependence.
  • Add the actual guidance algorithm (impact-point prediction à la ref [10]) — this thesis stops at the actuation layer.
  • A rig/alternator sized for unscaled flight spin.
  • Hardware-in-the-loop: feed kit position back into the flight sim so steering effects on trajectory close the loop, then test the guidance law with real actuator performance.
Best single paragraph to quote in a defense or interview: Section 6.1 — the authority envelope Taero,min = Tf = 0.1 N·m to Taero,max = (Kt·8 + Tf)/2 = 2.93 N·m, and Figure 7.4 — saturation points overlaying the velocity-controller output.
